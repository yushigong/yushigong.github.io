01OVERVIEW

Bringing DPoD into a more unified Thales product experience

DPoD is Thales’ cloud-based platform for deploying and managing cryptographic services that help organizations protect sensitive data and encryption keys.

As Thales’ product portfolio evolved, DPoD and Imperva had developed different visual languages, components, and interaction patterns. I led the redesign of DPoD using the shared Imperva design system—bringing the product into a more consistent visual language while making targeted usability improvements along the way.

02THE PROBLEM

DPoD no longer felt consistent with the broader product ecosystem

DPoD and Imperva had evolved with different visual languages, components, and interaction patterns. As Thales moved toward a more unified product experience, DPoD needed to align with the shared design direction.

The opportunity wasn’t to reinvent DPoD.It was to bring the product into the shared system without disrupting familiar enterprise workflows.

03AUDIT & OPPORTUNITIES

Not everything needed to be redesigned.

I audited DPoD’s core screens and workflows against Imperva’s shared design system to understand where the experience was simply inconsistent—and where users would actually benefit from a UX change.

06IMPACT

One redesign. A stronger shared product language.

The redesign did more than refresh DPoD visually. It brought the product closer to Imperva’s shared language, improved clarity across key workflows, and established a reusable foundation that Design and Engineering could continue building on instead of solving the same UI problems screen by screen.

Post-test surveys showed a 35% improvement in user satisfaction, while the reusable component approach was projected to deliver roughly a 40% efficiency gain by reducing repeated design and implementation work. Together, these changes gave DPoD a more consistent product language and a stronger foundation for future cross-product experiences.

WHAT I LEARNED

A successful redesign doesn’t have to reinvent everything.

The biggest lesson was learning to separate inconsistency from actual usability problems. Not every existing workflow needed a new interaction model; in many places, applying a stronger shared system was enough to make the experience clearer and more coherent.

I also saw how a design-system redesign can create value beyond visual consistency. Reusable components gave Design and Engineering a shared foundation, reduced repeated decisions, and made future product work easier to scale. The strongest outcome came from balancing system alignment with targeted UX improvements—changing what created real friction while preserving what users already understood.
